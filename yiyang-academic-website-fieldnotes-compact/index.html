About

I am a PhD candidate in Anthropology at Chinese University of Hong Kong. My research examines how people make sense of historical change through memory, material landscapes, institutions, and everyday cultural practices. I work across cultural anthropology, heritage and museum studies, urban studies, and digital ethnography.

My doctoral research draws on long-term ethnographic fieldwork in Lushun, northeastern China, to explore the contemporary lives of colonial pasts. Beyond this work, I study digital platforms and transnational encounters, and I am developing research on ruins, nostalgia, industrial decline, and the social afterlives of economic transformation.

My theoretical interests include hauntology and past presencing. I combine participant observation and interviews with digital ethnography, visual and textual analysis, and interdisciplinary approaches to culture and social change.

PERSONAL JOURNEY / THE ROOTS OF MY RESEARCH

Where do
we belong?

My interest in memory and belonging began long before I became an anthropologist.

I come from a family of industrial migrants. I grew up in an oilfield family, in an industrial town facing economic decline.

The oilfield was more than a workplace. For many families around me, it was a community shaped by the danwei (work unit), which connected employment, housing, welfare, and everyday life. Market reforms and the depletion of industrial resources transformed that world. Older generations remembered the collective life they had known, while many of my generation left to study and build careers in larger cities.

Growing up amid these changes made me attentive to the relationship between place, memory, and belonging. These questions accompanied me from museums and material culture to ethnographic research on colonial heritage, and continue to inform my interests in nostalgia, social transformation, and digital life.

For me, anthropology is a way to connect personal histories with broader social change.

Research

Doctoral research · Ethnography

Colonial heritage and its contemporary afterlives

Based on long-term fieldwork in Lushun, this project explores how colonial pasts remain active through heritage governance, commemorative practices, urban landscapes, and everyday life. It examines the uneven temporalities of preservation and the futures imagined for historic places.

Published research · Digital anthropology

Platform migration and transnational digital encounters

My research on TikTok users' migration to Xiaohongshu investigates platform-mediated encounters, cultural negotiation, and changing forms of digital belonging. It brings digital ethnography into dialogue with questions of mobility and social boundaries.

Collaborative research · Memory politics

Disaster memory and cultural governance

Collaborative work on the transformation of disaster memoryscapes in China examines how public memory is shaped through state narratives, local mediation, and civic participation.

Emerging research · Work in progress

Ruins, nostalgia, and post-socialist transformations

My emerging work considers urban exploration and the cultural meanings of abandoned spaces in contemporary China, alongside memories of industrial work, socialist institutions, and economic change. These interests connect questions of place and materiality to nostalgia, political economy, and imagined futures.
